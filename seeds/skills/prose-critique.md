## When to apply
During critic / revision passes on any draft. Provides an adversarial reading
methodology: read to find what fails, not to confirm what works.

## Technique

### Adversarial reading checklist
Read once for each of these dimensions, not all at once:

**1. Logic and causality**
- Does every scene/section follow from the one before it?
- Are character or argument decisions motivated? Could you explain why the character
  did that, or why the argument moved to this point?

**2. Specificity**
- Count generic adjectives (large, beautiful, important, significant). Each one is
  a missed opportunity for a specific detail.
- Flag any sentence that could appear unchanged in a different book or article.

**3. Pacing**
- Where does time dilate unnecessarily? (Three paragraphs on a trivial moment)
- Where does it compress too fast? (A key event summarized in one sentence)

**4. Voice consistency**
- Does the narrator's vocabulary, rhythm, and attention stay consistent?
- Are there sudden formal/informal register shifts that break immersion?

**5. Redundancy**
- Is any information stated twice? (Once in narrative, once in dialogue, etc.)
- Are there transitions that explain what the previous paragraph already showed?

**6. Tension**
- Does each section/chapter end with an open question or a turn?
- Is the central dramatic question still live at the midpoint?

### Filing critique notes
- Be specific: "paragraph 3 tells the reader she is nervous instead of showing a
  physical signal" not "show don't tell".
- Classify: BLOCKING (breaks logic, kills tension, contradicts established facts)
  vs. NIT (polish, preference, optional improvement).
- Do not soften findings. A polite critique is a useless one.

## Anti-pattern it replaces
Praise-first critique that buries findings. Surface-level feedback ("the writing
is good but could be more vivid") that gives the writer nothing to act on. Critique
that checks the wrong things (grammar over structure).
