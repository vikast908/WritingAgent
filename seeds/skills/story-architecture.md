## When to apply
Planning a book, article, or any long-form piece. Also when a chapter or section
feels structurally loose or fails to build toward anything.

## Technique

### Arc structure
- **Three-act shape (universal):** Setup (establish stakes + want) → Confrontation
  (escalating obstacles, want vs. need conflict) → Resolution (decisive action,
  transformation, answer). Works for fiction, essays, and long-form journalism.
- **The midpoint turn.** At roughly 50% of the piece, something must shift the
  protagonist/argument onto a new trajectory. Without it, the middle sags.
- **Setup → payoff contract.** Every significant element you introduce must pay off.
  Track open threads; close them deliberately or cut them.

### Tension management
- Tension = gap between what the reader wants and what they have. Maintain that gap.
- **Micro-tension** (sentence to sentence): unresolved questions, withheld information,
  implication over statement.
- **Macro-tension** (chapter to chapter): the central dramatic question must remain
  open until the final act.
- Releasing all tension at once (the "all is well" chapter) kills momentum.
  Partial resolution is more powerful: solve one problem, open a bigger one.

### Argument arc (for non-fiction / articles)
- **Thesis → evidence → complication → synthesis.** A flat "here are five points"
  structure has no arc. The complication - where you challenge your own thesis - is
  what makes the argument credible and the piece worth reading.
- Each section should advance the argument, not just add a related fact.
- The final section should leave the reader with something new, not just a summary
  of what was already said.

### Escalation
- Each act or section should raise the stakes of the central question.
- Repetition without escalation reads as padding. If a scene or section mirrors
  an earlier one without raising stakes, cut or merge it.

## Anti-pattern it replaces
Structurally flat writing where sections accumulate without building toward anything.
The "Wikipedia article" shape: topic → related fact → related fact → conclusion.
Arc requires transformation; accumulation does not.
