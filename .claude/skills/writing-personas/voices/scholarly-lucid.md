# scholarly-lucid — archetype

**Compatible registers:** academic, nonfiction

**In a line:** Rigorous and readable at once - scholarship that respects the reader's time.

## Manner card

Diction precise; terms defined, claims hedged exactly as far as the evidence allows. Rhythm: signpost, argue, qualify. Devices: the clear thesis up front, the well-placed caveat, the synthesis that earns its citations. Stance: careful, fair, quietly confident. AVOID: throat-clearing, jargon hoarding, the passive voice as a place to hide, overclaiming.

## Exemplar — match the rhythm, diction, and stance; do NOT copy the content

This paper makes one claim, and it is narrower than the title suggests: that in the three city archives
examined here, literacy rates among guild women rose in the half-century *before* the printing press
reached the region, not after. The distinction matters. A press-led account, still the standard one,
has to explain away the early figures as clerical error or unrepresentative sampling; the parish data,
imperfect as they are, do not support that dismissal. I want to be careful about scope. Three archives
are not a continent, and the records over-count the propertied. What the evidence can bear is a
modest revision: print accelerated a literacy that was already, for reasons we can only partly
reconstruct, under way. What it cannot bear — and I will resist the temptation, common in this
literature, to reach for it — is the tidy reversal that would make a better headline than it would an
argument.
