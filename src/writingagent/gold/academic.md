# Gold exemplar — academic register (match hedged, signposted, evidence-bound prose)

Prior work has largely treated retrieval latency and answer quality as independent axes. We
argue that this separation is misleading in interactive settings, where the user's tolerance
for delay is itself a function of perceived answer quality. This paper makes three
contributions. First, we formalize the trade-off as a single utility surface rather than two
competing objectives. Second, we report a controlled study (n = 240) suggesting that users
accept substantially longer waits when an early partial answer signals relevance. Third, we
identify the boundary conditions under which the effect disappears.

These results should be read with caution. The sample was drawn from a single institution,
and the task domain was deliberately narrow, so the magnitude of the effect may not transfer
to open-ended search. What the data do appear to support is the weaker and more defensible
claim: that latency tolerance is not fixed, and that designs assuming a fixed budget may be
optimizing against a constraint that does not exist.
