## When to apply
Any non-fiction or technical section that makes factual claims: statistics, causal
statements, comparisons, "best practice" assertions, or historical facts.

## Technique

### Every claim earns its evidence
- A factual claim needs a specific source, a named example, a measurement, or a
  reproducible demonstration. If you cannot supply one, soften the claim to an
  opinion ("in my experience") or cut it.
- Cite inline at the point of the claim, not in a footnote the reader skips: "X grew
  40% in 2023 [3]" with [3] resolving to a real, dated source in the references.

### Separate fact, inference, and opinion
- Mark which is which. "The benchmark ran 3x faster" (fact) is different from
  "so the new allocator is better" (inference) is different from "I would default to
  it" (opinion). Conflating them reads as manipulation.

### Prefer primary and specific over secondary and vague
- Cite the spec, the paper, the source code, or the measurement - not a blog post
  summarizing it. Name the version, the date, the conditions.
- Replace "studies show" / "experts agree" / "it is widely known" with the actual
  study, the named expert, or the specific reason. Vague attribution is the tell of
  a claim with no real source.

### No invented evidence
- Never fabricate a statistic, quote, study, date, or case study to make a point land.
  A made-up "73% of teams" is worse than no number. If the real figure is unknown,
  say the direction without the false precision.

### Quantify where you can
- "Faster" is weak; "12ms vs 40ms on a 10k-row table" is evidence. Specific numbers,
  with their conditions, beat comparative adjectives.

## Anti-pattern it replaces
Confident assertions with no backing, "studies show" hand-waving, fabricated or
unsourced statistics, opinion dressed as established fact, and comparative claims
("faster", "more secure") with no measurement behind them.
